SNICTalk: Biologically Inspired SNIC Oscillator Dynamics for Temporally Coherent Speech-Driven 3D Facial Animation

Farzaneh Jafari1, and Anup Basu1
1University of Alberta, Edmonton, AB, Canada
Paper Video Code arXiv

A phase oscillator whose geometry encodes rest: SNICTalk on BIWI and VOCASET.

Abstract

Speech-driven 3D facial animation requires accurate vertex prediction together with realistic temporal articulation. Beyond lip synchronization, facial motion must exhibit natural periods of rest interleaved with rapid articulatory events. Existing approaches are trained with mean-squared error objectives that consistently converge toward over-smoothed average trajectories that lack genuine rest states between phonemes, a failure rooted in the absence of any structural prior for the rhythmic organization of articulatory motion. Inspired by cardiac pacemaker dynamics, we propose a biologically inspired mechanism that uses the same mathematical substrate, the Saddle-Node on Invariant Circle (SNIC) bifurcation, in which a limit cycle is formed by the collision of a stable rest state and a threshold on a closed orbit. The geometric consequence is a ghost zone where the orbit dwells arbitrarily long near the annihilated rest state, providing precisely the structural prior that sequence models lack, a natural attractor encoding closed-mouth silence, whose dwell time scales continuously with audio drive, from complete rest to rapid articulation. Extensive experiments on BIWI and VOCASET demonstrate that SNICTalk consistently achieves state-of-the-art geometric accuracy and motion dynamics diversity, producing facial motion with natural rest-articulation transitions rather than over-smoothed averages.

Why does facial motion need a rest state?

Current methods treat speech-driven facial animation as sequence regression, trained end-to-end with per-frame MSE. MSE training converges toward the conditional mean of the vertex distribution at each frame, which suppresses the high-variance components of motion, the sharp onset of a vowel, the rapid release of a plosive, and the return to true rest during silence. There is no explicit structural mechanism for the face to return to its neutral template during silence, because the average prediction over silent training examples is typically a small non-zero displacement rather than the neutral template.

We take our answer from cardiac electrophysiology. Sinoatrial pacemaker cells operate near a Saddle-Node on Invariant Circle (SNIC) bifurcation, in which a stable rest state and a threshold coexist as fixed points on a closed orbit. As a continuous drive parameter crosses a critical value, the two fixed points collide and annihilate, releasing the orbit, but the trajectory still slows dramatically near the location of the annihilated rest state, the ghost zone. We argue this matches the dynamical structure of speech. The ghost zone is the closed-mouth neutral face during silence, and the fast arc of the orbit is the articulatory cycle of a phoneme.

Method

Fig. 1. Raw audio is encoded by a frozen Wav2Vec2 encoder and aligned to the target frame rate. The shared audio representation is processed by three complementary branches: a Mamba-based geometry backbone, EnvelopeSNIC, and CoherenceSNIC, whose outputs are fused additively and combined with the neutral template to produce the final animated 3D face.

SNICTalk is a three-path architecture. Path 1 (Mamba backbone) is a selective state-space model with LayerNorm and a GELU feed-forward network, providing accurate per-frame vertex positions but no genuine rest states on its own. Path 2 (EnvelopeSNIC) is a single-phase SNIC oscillator over the full mesh that decides whether the face is mid-articulation or at rest, giving a geometric inductive prior for the temporal envelope of speech. Path 3 (CoherenceSNIC) is an eight-oscillator, gap-junction-coupled ensemble operating exclusively on lip vertices, synchronized the way sinoatrial pacemaker cells synchronize, to capture fine-scale, spatially coherent phoneme-specific articulation.

The ghost zone, traced through a word

Fig. 2. Illustration of the SNIC oscillator dynamics underlying SNICTalk's articulatory event detection for the utterance “ball.” The oscillator's input current I(t) is parked near the ghost of the saddle-node fixed point during silence, crosses the bifurcation threshold ISNIC at the bilabial burst, circulates through the open-vowel peak, and re-parks at post-utterance silence — returning the face to its neutral template without any explicit rest-state loss.

When the audio drive falls below the bifurcation threshold, the phase orbit dwells at the ghost of the annihilated rest state, with dwell time scaling as τghost ∝ 1/√(I − ISNIC). This diverges as the drive approaches threshold from above, letting the oscillator idle indefinitely during silence while still transitioning continuously into rapid articulation — unlike a Hopf oscillator, which bifurcates immediately to a non-zero onset frequency and cannot represent genuine stillness without an external, learned gating mechanism.

CoherenceSNIC: coupled oscillators on the lip region

Fig. 3. The shared audio drive I(t) conditions an ensemble of eight coupled oscillators distributed around the lip contour. Solid lines denote local coupling between adjacent oscillators; dashed lines denote long-range coupling between opposing oscillators. The resulting correction is gated and applied exclusively to lip vertices, with a Gaussian boundary weight blending it smoothly into the surrounding face.

Gap-junction coupling between adjacent pacemaker cells synchronizes phase through a resistive current proportional to phase difference. We implement this as multi-scale diffusion coupling — dilation scales {1, 2, 4} — between the eight oscillators, giving lip vertex motion that is coherent across the lip region while retaining enough phase diversity to represent the distinct kinematics of the upper lip, lower lip, and lip corners.

Quantitative Results

SNICTalk achieves the best MVE, LVE, FDD, and FFE on BIWI6 and VOCASET against nine state-of-the-art baselines, indicating that the SNIC-based inductive prior improves both static reconstruction accuracy and motion diversity rather than trading one for the other.

Path-Level Ablation

Removing Path 2 (EnvelopeSNIC) leaves geometric accuracy and temporal quality largely intact, even improving MOD, VE, AE, and TC, but costs FDD and FFE. Removing Path 3 (CoherenceSNIC) shows the reverse pattern in part, winning MOD, AE, and FFE outright while losing ground on VE, TC, and FDD. Neither ablated variant dominates the full model — evidence that the two paths target genuinely different aspects of facial motion: rest-timing and spatial diversity, respectively.
